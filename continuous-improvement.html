Ongoing Themes

Some things don’t get solved once and filed away. These are the topics that the programme and the organisation need to keep returning to, discussing across teams, and improving iteratively over time. Each theme is a live question, not a closed action.

How to Use This Page

Each theme captures a problem space that the digital transformation can help address — but only if we work on it deliberately and across the organisation. Use these themes to frame conversations, commission discovery work, benchmark against peers, and track progress over time. Add new themes as they emerge. Return to existing ones as the programme matures.

Themes are not project plans. They are structured thinking about where we should be improving, the questions we haven’t fully answered, and what others are doing that we might learn from.

Staff & Tenant Safety

Digital transformation should make the working environment safer for housing staff, reduce safety risks in tenant properties, and create auditable evidence that safety obligations are being met. Many of the checks and records that protect people are still paper-based, intermittent, or inaccessible in the field — and that is a risk that the programme can directly address.

Improvement Areas

PPE Compliance & Issuing

PPE issuing is often recorded informally or not at all. Staff may not have the right equipment for specific property types, and there is no automatic link between job type and required PPE.

  • Digital PPE records per operative — what was issued, when, and by whom
  • Automatic prompts on job dispatch: if a property has an asbestos survey flag, require acknowledgement of appropriate PPE before the job is confirmed
  • Expiry tracking and automated alerts for items with renewal dates (e.g. hard hats, harnesses, respiratory equipment)
  • Mobile sign-off when PPE is collected, replacing paper log books in the depot

Mobile Compliance Forms & Regulation Sign-off

Risk assessments, COSHH records, method statements, and regulatory sign-offs are still handled on paper in many areas. Operatives and tenants should be able to sign and receive documents on a mobile device at point of work.

  • RAMS (Risk Assessment and Method Statements) completed and signed on mobile before work begins
  • Tenant sign-off for access, gas safety notices, and damp treatment consent — captured digitally at the door
  • Completed forms automatically attached to the property record — no scanning, no lost paperwork
  • Offline capability so operatives can complete forms in properties with no signal

Property Safety Records in the Field

Operatives arrive at properties without access to asbestos surveys, dampness histories, or structural notes. This is a direct safety risk and also a liability risk for the organisation.

  • Asbestos register surfaced in the operative’s app before they enter a property — not buried in a back-office system
  • Dampness and mould history visible per property so operatives understand the context of the repair
  • Property-level hazard flags (e.g. aggressive dog, access difficulties, safeguarding notes) shown on job dispatch
  • Automatic prompts when a job type interacts with a known property hazard (e.g. drilling near a flagged asbestos area)

Automated Operational Audits

Van checks, stock checks, equipment calibration records, and tool inspections are currently done on paper clipboards or not consistently done at all. The system should make these automatic, timestamped, and undeniable.

  • Daily van inspection completed on the operative’s device before the first job — not a paper form in the glove box
  • Stock checks and materials reconciliation done on mobile at the depot, with automatic discrepancy alerts
  • Equipment calibration schedules tracked digitally — the system flags when a tool is overdue for inspection
  • Audit trail available to supervisors in real time, not reconstructed from paper records after an incident
  • Certificates and compliance documents (gas safety, electrical, legionella) tracked against renewal dates with automated reminders

Lone Worker Wellbeing

Staff working alone should feel supported, not monitored. The conversation about what digital tools can offer here needs to happen with staff and unions, not be decided for them. The starting point is understanding what staff themselves feel would help — and building from there collaboratively.

  • Talk to staff first — a discovery conversation about what situations feel uncomfortable and what kind of support would genuinely help is the right starting point before any tool is chosen
  • Simple, voluntary check-in options that staff can use if they want to — not mandatory timed prompts that feel like surveillance
  • An easy way to flag a concern or request support through the app, designed around staff comfort rather than organisational process
  • Making it easier to report incidents or near-misses without bureaucratic friction — so the organisation learns and improves, and staff feel heard
  • Any broader capability (such as a personal safety alert) introduced only through proper staff and union engagement, with clear boundaries on what data is held and by whom

How Do Others Approach This?

Wheatley Housing Group (Glasgow)

Scotland’s largest housing organisation uses Totalmobile for job management, with van inspection logs, PPE acknowledgement steps, and compliance checks built into the operative workflow. Safety flags from the property record surface automatically before job confirmation. They credit the system with a significant reduction in near-miss incidents by operatives entering properties without hazard awareness.

Mears Group (national contractor, multiple Scottish councils)

Mears operate digital RAMS sign-off at job commencement, with tenant and operative signatures captured on the operative’s device. Risk assessments are pre-populated from the property hazard record and amended in the field if conditions differ from the file. Completed forms are uploaded to the job record in real time — no manual filing.

Sanctuary Group (England)

Implemented Peoplesafe lone worker monitoring integrated with their housing management system. Lone worker sessions are started and ended through the operative app; overdue check-ins escalate automatically to the on-call supervisor. Post-incident reviews showed that the structured session approach also improved reporting discipline across the workforce.

Riverside Group (North West England)

Property-level safety data — asbestos, structural, dampness — is surfaced in the field app before job dispatch, not just available to back-office staff. Operatives must acknowledge hazard flags before the job opens. This was introduced after an asbestos exposure incident where the operative had not been informed of a survey result held in a separate system.

Scottish Housing Regulator — Annual Assurance Statement

The SHR now requires landlords to certify compliance with all safety obligations as part of the Annual Assurance process. Landlords with digital audit trails — showing that checks were done, certificates are current, and operatives were informed of hazards — are significantly better placed to provide that assurance. Paper-based systems create gaps that are difficult to defend in a regulatory review.

Questions to Keep Asking

  1. What does an operative genuinely need to know before entering a property to do their job safely — and does the current system surface that information at the right moment?
  2. Which paper safety checks are being done consistently, and which are being done intermittently or skipped? How would we know if we didn’t ask?
  3. How do we know PPE is actually being used, not just issued? What evidence could we produce if there was an incident?
  4. What is the current lag between a safety concern being identified in a property (e.g. mould, structural crack, electrical hazard) and it being recorded in a way that protects both the tenant and the organisation?
  5. Could a lone worker incident happen today and go unnoticed for two hours or more? What would it take to change that?
  6. Are tenants receiving all the safety information they are entitled to — gas safety certificates, damp treatment plans, asbestos notifications — and do we have consistent evidence of that?
  7. When the Scottish Housing Regulator asks for assurance on safety compliance, what is our current answer and how much of it relies on paper that could be lost or incomplete?

Data, Integration & Contract Pricing

The new system landscape has clear ownership: NEC Housing is the truth for properties and tenants, ROCC is the truth for repairs and includes the contract and pricing module, and Integra is the finance system that job costs and invoicing flow into. There are two main integration boundaries to design carefully: NEC ↔ ROCC, and ROCC ↔ Integra. What ERC also needs to take control of is the contract pricing data from Scotland Excel frameworks and Renfrew trade contracts — ensuring it arrives in ROCC as structured, machine-readable data rather than spreadsheets and PDFs.

Improvement Areas

Schema Simplicity & Type Discipline

The simplest schema that correctly models the domain is always preferable to one that tries to anticipate every future need. Every field in the database should have a reason to exist, a defined type, and a constraint where appropriate.

  • Use the most specific data type available: DATE not VARCHAR for dates, DECIMAL(10,2) not FLOAT for prices, BOOLEAN not TINYINT for flags
  • Apply NOT NULL constraints on every field that must always exist — do not default to nullable columns “just in case”
  • Use ENUM or a reference table for any field with a fixed set of valid values (status, type, category) — never free text for controlled vocabularies
  • Every table should have a surrogate primary key, a created_at timestamp, and an updated_at timestamp as standard
  • Foreign key constraints enforced at the database level, not just in application code
  • Soft deletes (deleted_at) rather than hard deletes for any record with audit or compliance significance (tenants, repairs, staff, properties)

Address Standardisation via Gazetteer & UPRN

ERC uses the Local Land and Property Gazetteer (LLPG) as its authoritative address register, accessible through the Improvement Service’s Spatial Hub Scotland. Every property in the housing stock has a Unique Property Reference Number (UPRN) — a 12-digit identifier that should become the primary key for all property records across every system.

  • UPRN stored as BIGINT on every property record — this is the join key between housing management, repairs, SOR, tenant portal, and any future system
  • Address display generated from Gazetteer data at query time, not stored as a free-text string that can drift out of date
  • Legacy address strings in the current system matched to UPRN during migration — use OpenAI Structured Outputs to parse ambiguous or abbreviated addresses and suggest UPRN candidates for human confirmation
  • Any new address entered into the new system validated against the live Gazetteer feed before being committed — no unmatched addresses accepted
  • Postcode stored separately in consistent format: uppercase, space before the inward code (G77 5NE), validated against the Royal Mail PAF

System Integration: NEC ↔ ROCC ↔ Integra

Two integration boundaries, designed well from the start, prevent years of manual workarounds. UPRN is the shared key between NEC and ROCC — every data exchange must be built around it. Integra receives the financial output of completed work.

  • NEC → ROCC: When a repair is raised, ROCC needs property details (UPRN, address, property type, tenure), the tenant reference for notification, and any property hazard flags — pulled live from NEC via API, not copied into a local table that can drift
  • ROCC → NEC: Repair history and job status written back to the tenant record in NEC so staff don’t need to check two systems for a tenant’s repair history
  • ROCC → Integra: Completed job data (SOR codes, quantities, costs, operative, date) sent to Integra for invoicing and budget management — not re-keyed by someone manually entering job sheets into the finance system
  • Integra → ROCC: Budget codes, approved supplier list, and purchase order confirmations available in ROCC so operatives and supervisors have the financial context they need without switching systems
  • Every integration uses JSON over a documented API — no file drops, no shared folders, no scheduled CSV exports between systems
  • Define a data ownership matrix before build: for every field that appears in more than one system, one system is the master and the others are consumers — never two systems writing the same field independently

Contract Pricing Data: Scotland Excel & Renfrew Trade Contracts

This is the data ERC genuinely owns and must control. Scotland Excel framework rates and Renfrew trade contract schedules are the pricing basis for repairs and maintenance work. They currently arrive as spreadsheets and PDFs — documents designed for human reading, not machine ingestion. Bringing this data under proper version control and making it available as structured, queryable data is one of the highest-value data problems the programme can solve.

  • Scotland Excel frameworks: Rate schedules published by Scotland Excel for participating councils need to be ingested as structured data, not stored as spreadsheet files. Each SOR code, description, unit of measure, and unit rate is a database record, not a cell in a tab on a workbook
  • Renfrew trade contracts: ERC’s participation in joint contracts with Renfrewshire and other authorities gives access to negotiated rates — these schedules need to be maintained as live data in the system so ROCC can use them for job costing without manual lookups
  • SOR code standardisation: SOR codes across different contracts may use different coding conventions; a mapping table is needed so ROCC can work with a consistent internal code regardless of which contract the rate comes from
  • Rate versioning: Contract rates change at renewal; the system must store the effective date range for each rate so historical jobs are costed against the rate that applied at the time — not silently updated to the current rate
  • Price data types: All monetary values stored as DECIMAL(10,2) — never FLOAT; unit of measure as a typed ENUM (EA, M, M2, HR); currency as ISO 4217 (GBP) even if it seems unnecessary now
  • Staff data (ERC-owned): Operative trade classification, certifications with expiry dates, and van/equipment assignments are ERC data — stored as structured records with typed fields, not managed in a spreadsheet or HR system notes field

OpenAI-Assisted Migration & Cleansing

The OpenAI API — specifically Structured Outputs with a defined JSON Schema — is the most practical tool available for transforming legacy records that are too inconsistent to fix with deterministic rules alone. The key is treating AI as a preprocessing step that produces structured candidates for human confirmation, not as an autonomous data editor.

  • Use the OpenAI Batch API to process large contract schedules overnight: send a row from a Scotland Excel spreadsheet, receive a structured JSON object matching the internal SOR schema — with confidence score and any ambiguities flagged for review
  • Define a JSON Schema for the SOR record before starting — Structured Outputs guarantees the AI returns valid JSON that matches it, making batch ingest safe to automate
  • SOR code parsing: send a legacy or supplier-formatted code string → receive { "sor_code": "PL0042", "description": "Replace isolating valve", "unit": "EA", "confidence": 0.97 } → flag anything below 0.9 for manual review
  • Address normalisation (for any legacy records that still need it): send a free-text address string → receive { "uprn": 12345678, "confidence": 0.94, "matched_address": "12 High Street, Barrhead, G78 1AA" }
  • Contract schedule comparison: use AI to diff two versions of a rate schedule and identify which SOR codes changed, were added, or were removed — surfaces the delta automatically rather than asking someone to compare two spreadsheets by eye
  • Migration audit log: every AI-suggested change recorded with the original value, the suggested value, the confidence score, and whether it was accepted or overridden — this log is part of the compliance record

JSON-First Integration Architecture

Every integration between systems should use structured JSON over a REST or webhook API. The goal is that no data ever travels via a spreadsheet attached to an email, a shared network folder, or a scheduled CSV export — because those channels cannot be monitored, versioned, or automatically validated.

  • All APIs accept and return JSON with a defined schema — document with OpenAPI 3.0 spec so any future developer or system can consume without asking
  • Event-driven webhooks for state changes (job completed, tenant updated, certificate expired) rather than polling or scheduled exports
  • JSON Schema validation at the API boundary: reject malformed payloads at ingestion, not after they have corrupted a table
  • Versioned API endpoints (/api/v1/) so integrations do not break when the schema evolves
  • Idempotent endpoints: sending the same payload twice produces the same result — safe for retry on network failure
  • Structured logs for every API call: request body, response, timestamp, duration — makes debugging and auditing straightforward

Eliminating Manual File Transfers & Spreadsheet Workflows

Every spreadsheet that travels between systems or people via email is a data quality incident waiting to happen: it can be edited, forwarded to the wrong person, become the “live” version without anyone knowing, and is impossible to audit. The goal is to make the new system the authoritative source so that no one needs a spreadsheet because the data is always one click away.

  • Map every spreadsheet that currently carries contract or pricing data: Scotland Excel schedules, Renfrew trade rates, materials price lists — each one is a workflow to replace with a structured import or API
  • When Scotland Excel publishes a revised framework schedule, the process should be: download structured file → validate against schema → import to contract pricing table → diff against previous version → notify affected users. Not: download Excel → email around for comment → manually update a local copy
  • Self-service reports in ROCC replace “could you send me the list of…” emails — role-based access means each user sees the data relevant to them without an intermediary
  • Staff certification data (gas safe, NICEIC, asbestos awareness, etc.) tracked in the system with automated expiry alerts — not managed in a spreadsheet by one person who may be on leave when a certificate lapses
  • Power Automate or a lightweight job scheduler for genuinely batch processes: nightly reconciliation between ROCC job costs and contract rates, weekly SLA summary, monthly Scotland Excel compliance reporting — triggered automatically
  • Change log on every pricing record: who changed what, when, and from which contract — essential for audit when a job cost is queried months later

How Do Others Approach This?

Improvement Service — Spatial Hub Scotland (national)

The Improvement Service provides all Scottish councils with access to the National Gazetteer through the Spatial Hub. ERC already has LLPG obligations as a local authority. The practical step is ensuring the UPRN is exposed as a field in the new housing system and that the Spatial Hub API is called at address entry time rather than relying on a periodic bulk import. Several Scottish councils — including North Lanarkshire and South Ayrshire — have completed UPRN adoption programmes that reduced address duplication by over 30% and improved integration between housing, planning, and environmental services.

Aberdeenshire Council — housing data migration

Aberdeenshire’s housing system migration identified that approximately 22% of tenant records had phone numbers in inconsistent formats, 14% had addresses not matched to UPRN, and 8% were probable duplicates. They used a combination of deterministic rules and manual review — a process that took three months longer than planned. The lesson they cite: start the data quality audit at the same time as the procurement process, not after contract award. Having a clean data profile early changes what you ask for in a specification.

Riverside Group — API-first integration strategy

Riverside eliminated approximately 40 recurring spreadsheet-based workflows over three years by requiring every new system integration to use their internal API gateway. The discipline they applied: any data that moves between systems must go through the API; anything that cannot go through the API is a project to add that capability, not a reason to revert to email. They report that data quality incidents dropped by 60% and that their compliance reporting workload reduced significantly because the audit trail was always available in the system.

NHS Scotland — CHI number discipline

The Community Health Index (CHI) number is the NHS Scotland universal patient identifier — analogous in function to the UPRN for properties or a tenancy reference for tenants. Every NHS system uses CHI as the primary key for patient records, which means that a patient seen in A&E, a GP practice, and a district nursing team has one consistent identity across all three. The lesson for housing is the same: pick a canonical identifier for each entity (UPRN for properties, tenancy reference for tenancies) and enforce it across every system from day one. Adding it later costs significantly more.

OpenAI Structured Outputs — data migration pattern

Several UK local authorities and housing associations piloting AI-assisted data migration (including a Midlands housing association and a Scottish council, both reported at the 2024 Chartered Institute of Housing digital conference) used GPT-4 with Structured Outputs to pre-process legacy records before human review. The typical pattern: define a JSON Schema for the target record, batch-process 500–1,000 records per API call overnight, review the flagged low-confidence results in the morning. One team reported processing 85,000 tenant records in four working days with a 94% auto-acceptance rate, leaving 5,100 records for manual review — work that would have taken several weeks by hand.

Questions to Keep Asking

  1. Have we defined the data ownership matrix? For every field that appears in NEC, ROCC, and Integra, which system is the master and which are consumers?
  2. What is the current state of Scotland Excel and Renfrew contract rate data? Where does it live, in what format, and who is responsible for keeping it current?
  3. Which recurring spreadsheets carry contract or pricing data right now — who creates them, how often, and what decision they drive? Each one is a workflow to replace.
  4. When Scotland Excel publishes a revised framework schedule, what is the current journey from publication to the rates being live in the system used for job costing? How many manual steps are involved?
  5. Are SOR codes consistent between Scotland Excel schedules and Renfrew trade contracts, or do we need a mapping layer? Has anyone compared them?
  6. What data does ROCC need from NEC every time a repair is raised, and is the NEC API capable of supplying it? Has this been confirmed with the NEC supplier, not just assumed?
  7. Are we treating the JSON schema for each integration boundary as a design document agreed before build starts — or something defined after the systems are built and then retrofitted?
  8. When a contract rate changes, how will historical jobs be protected from having their costs silently updated? Is rate versioning in the ROCC data model from day one?

Void Turnover Improvements

Every day a property sits empty has a direct financial cost and means a household on the waiting list goes without a home. Void turnaround time is one of the most visible performance indicators in Scottish housing — measured against the Scottish Housing Charter, watched by the regulator, and felt acutely by applicants. Digital transformation can reduce turnaround time by removing the friction between each stage of the void process: inspection, works ordering, completion, and re-let.

Improvement Areas

Digital Void Inspection

The void inspection is the starting point for everything that follows. Done on a paper form, the information sits in someone’s hand or in-tray before it reaches the people who need it. Done digitally on a mobile device, it is available to the repairs team the moment the inspector submits.

  • Mobile inspection form completed on site — room-by-room condition grading, photos attached directly to the property record
  • Inspection result immediately visible in ROCC, eliminating the delay between inspection and works scheduling
  • Standard defect codes used consistently across all void inspections so data can be aggregated and patterns identified
  • Photos timestamped and geotagged — useful for insurance, disputes, and condition benchmarking over time

Automatic Works Scheduling

The biggest delay in most void processes is the gap between inspection and works being scheduled. This should be near-zero — inspection findings map directly to ROCC works orders without a manual handoff.

  • Inspection defect codes map to standard SOR works items in ROCC — the inspection generates a draft works schedule automatically
  • Works prioritised by category: statutory (gas, electrical safety) first, then cosmetic
  • Works schedule visible to the allocations team in NEC so re-let planning can begin in parallel with works, not after
  • Completion sign-off by the operative triggers a notification to the allocations team that the property is ready

Void KPI Visibility

If managers can only see void performance at month-end, problems that developed in week one are only visible in week five. Real-time visibility changes that.

  • Live void dashboard: number of current voids, days at each stage, flag for any void exceeding target turnaround
  • Average void turnaround shown against the Scottish Housing Charter indicator and the previous year — trend visible at a glance
  • Early warning when a void is approaching the target without works being completed — intervention before the breach, not a report afterwards
  • Void cost calculated automatically from ROCC job costs — rent loss and works cost visible together

How Do Others Approach This?

Scottish Housing Regulator — Charter Indicator 8

The SHR measures void management under Charter Indicator 8: the average number of calendar days taken to re-let properties. ERC’s performance is published annually and compared against Scottish averages. Digital inspection and automatic works scheduling are cited in multiple ARC submissions by higher-performing landlords as the primary driver of turnaround improvement.

Wheatley Housing Group

Wheatley use a mobile void inspection app that integrates directly with their repairs scheduling system. Inspection to first trade on site in under 24 hours for standard voids is their operational target. They attribute this to eliminating the paper-based handoff between the inspector and the works scheduler entirely.

North Lanarkshire Council

Moved to digital void inspections with automatic SOR schedule generation in their ROCC implementation. Reported a reduction in average void turnaround from 28 days to 19 days in the first year, primarily through removing the delay between inspection completion and works ordering.

Questions to Keep Asking

  1. What is our current average void turnaround time and how does it compare to the Scottish Housing Charter average? Where do we sit in the national picture?
  2. At which stage does a void most commonly get delayed — inspection, works scheduling, works completion, or sign-off? Do we actually know, or are we guessing?
  3. How long currently passes between an inspection being completed and a works order being raised? Who is responsible for that handoff?
  4. Can the allocations team in NEC see the current status of void works in real time, or do they have to ask someone?
  5. How many void properties are currently being tracked in a spreadsheet or on a whiteboard rather than in a system?

Stock Management Improvements

Stock management operates at two levels that are often treated separately but are deeply connected: the condition and lifecycle of the housing stock itself (the properties), and the operational materials and parts needed to maintain it. Weak data at either level drives reactive, expensive decisions. Good data at both enables planned, efficient maintenance and a clearer picture of the organisation’s long-term capital obligations.

Improvement Areas

Housing Stock Condition

Knowing the condition of every property in the stock is the foundation of planned maintenance. Without it, the organisation is always reacting. With it, expenditure can be forecast, programmes can be planned, and the Scottish Housing Quality Standard can be demonstrated systematically.

  • Digital condition surveys completed on mobile, with photos and standardised element grades linked directly to the property UPRN
  • Component-level asset register: each property’s boiler, windows, roof, kitchen, and bathroom recorded with installation date and expected replacement year
  • Survey data feeds directly into a planned maintenance programme — components approaching end of life automatically appear in the forward works plan
  • SHQS (Scottish Housing Quality Standard) compliance status calculated from survey data, not assembled manually from multiple spreadsheets

Van Stock & Materials Management

Operatives carrying the wrong stock, or not enough of what they need, drives repeat visits and increases cost per job. Van stock management is a practical, high-impact area where digital tools can make an immediate difference to first-time fix rates.

  • Van stock recorded digitally: what each operative is carrying, consumed automatically when a job is completed and a part is used
  • Low stock alerts sent to the operative and supervisor before the operative runs out — not after a job is left incomplete
  • Replenishment request raised through the app, not via a phone call to the depot
  • Stock usage data analysed to optimise what each van carries by trade, season, and area — reducing unnecessary carrying costs and emergency runs

Reactive vs Planned Maintenance Balance

A housing organisation spending most of its maintenance budget on reactive repairs is in a cycle it cannot easily break. Every pound spent on planned maintenance based on good stock condition data prevents several pounds of reactive spend. This is well evidenced across the sector and worth tracking explicitly.

  • Track the reactive/planned split in ROCC by value and by job count — visible to management monthly
  • Set a target trajectory: where should the reactive/planned ratio be in 1, 3, and 5 years?
  • Link stock condition survey data to the repairs history for each property — identify properties generating disproportionate reactive spend as candidates for planned investment
  • Use Scotland Excel framework contracts to plan and procure bulk replacement programmes efficiently

How Do Others Approach This?

Aberdeenshire Council

Completed a full stock condition survey programme linked to their housing management system, enabling them to produce a 30-year capital expenditure forecast and demonstrate SHQS compliance systematically. They report that the survey data also significantly improved their ability to plan void works because known component conditions could be factored in before the property became empty.

Mears Group (operating for multiple Scottish councils)

Mears use digital van stock management integrated with their job management system. Parts consumed on a job are deducted from van stock automatically at job completion. The system generates replenishment orders nightly based on usage patterns and upcoming scheduled work, reducing emergency procurement by a reported 35%.

Wheatley Housing Group

Wheatley track their reactive/planned maintenance ratio as a board-level KPI and set an annual improvement target. Their move from roughly 70% reactive / 30% planned to closer to 50/50 over five years is attributed to investment in stock condition data and the planned maintenance programmes it enabled, rather than to any single technology.

Questions to Keep Asking

  1. Do we have a complete, up-to-date component-level asset register for all properties? If not, what would it take to create one?
  2. What is our current reactive/planned maintenance ratio by value? Is it improving year on year?
  3. How does an operative know what stock they have on the van before they leave the depot in the morning?
  4. Which properties are generating the most reactive repairs? Are those properties candidates for planned investment?
  5. Are we able to demonstrate SHQS compliance for every property from system data, or does someone have to assemble evidence manually?

Tenant Engagement & Communication

Tenant engagement is the hardest thing to do well and the most important. Past experience in the organisation shows that meaningful, sustained engagement is genuinely difficult to achieve. But the insight it generates is qualitatively different from any other source — tenants know what it is actually like to live in a property, report a repair, wait for it to be done, and deal with the outcomes when it goes wrong. Every improvement shaped by genuine tenant feedback lands better, lasts longer, and builds more trust than one that doesn’t. This theme should be treated as more active and more urgent than the others precisely because it is harder and has been underinvested before.

Why This Theme Is Different

Tenant input defines what quality actually means across the whole programme — not what we think it means from behind a desk. The goal is not to collect feedback and file it. It is to close the loop visibly, so tenants can see that their input changed something. That is what builds the trust that makes future engagement possible.

Improvement Areas

Repairs Satisfaction & Post-Job Feedback

The moment a repair is completed is the highest-quality moment to ask for feedback. The experience is fresh, the outcome is clear, and the tenant has a concrete basis for a response. This feedback is also the most directly actionable — it identifies specific operatives, job types, and processes that are working well or not.

  • Automated satisfaction request sent by SMS or portal notification immediately after job completion — not a quarterly survey
  • Short, specific questions: did the operative arrive in the time slot given? Was the repair completed in one visit? Was the property left clean?
  • Low scores trigger a follow-up call from a supervisor within 48 hours — not a logged statistic
  • Satisfaction data visible by operative, trade, and job type so patterns can be identified and addressed

Proactive Communication

The most common reason tenants contact the housing service is to chase the status of something they have already reported. Every one of those calls or messages is a failure of proactive communication, and it costs staff time as well as tenant goodwill. The new system should make “where is my repair?” an unnecessary question.

  • Automated status updates at every meaningful stage: repair logged, appointment booked, operative on the way, job completed
  • Appointment reminders sent the day before and the morning of — reducing missed appointments which are a significant cost
  • If a repair is delayed, the tenant is told proactively with a revised timescale — not left to wonder
  • Communication by the tenant’s preferred channel (SMS, portal, email) stored in NEC as a preference — not defaulted to one channel for everyone

Scrutiny, Co-design & Closing the Loop

Tenant scrutiny — where tenants independently review and challenge how services are delivered — is a requirement of the Scottish Social Housing Charter and, done well, is one of the most powerful tools for service improvement. Co-design goes further: tenants shape what is built before it is built. Both require genuine commitment from the organisation, not a consultation exercise.

  • Involve tenants in the design of the repairs portal and notification system before build — not as testers of a finished product
  • Tenant scrutiny panel given access to real performance data (satisfaction scores, void turnaround, first-time fix rate) so their reviews are based on evidence
  • Every formal engagement produces a “you said, we did” response published on the tenant portal — the most important step in building credibility for future engagement
  • Engagement accessible to all tenants, including those who are elderly, digitally excluded, or have communication support needs — not defaulting to online surveys that exclude the people most likely to have the strongest views

Understanding What Tenants Are Telling Us

Feedback arrives in many forms: satisfaction scores, complaint records, repair descriptions, portal messages, scrutiny panel notes, phone call logs. Individually they are fragments. Analysed together they reveal patterns that no single channel would surface alone.

  • Use OpenAI to analyse unstructured feedback text at scale — identifying recurring themes, sentiment patterns, and service areas generating the most dissatisfaction
  • Complaints data analysed alongside satisfaction scores — properties, trades, or processes generating both low satisfaction and high complaints are the priority
  • Analysis shared with the tenant scrutiny panel as part of their evidence pack — not just held internally
  • Feedback from digitally excluded tenants (phone, face-to-face) captured and coded to the same schema so it is not systematically absent from the analysis

How Do Others Approach This?

Scottish Social Housing Charter — Outcome 3

The Charter requires landlords to demonstrate that they actively seek and act on tenants’ views, and that tenants find it easy to participate in decisions about services. The ARS (Annual Return on the Charter) measures tenant satisfaction with repairs, landlord communication, and the landlord’s response to their views. ERC’s published scores and trajectory are part of the public record.

Wheatley Housing Group — Tenant Scrutiny

Wheatley’s tenant scrutiny panels are given access to live performance dashboards and commission their own service reviews. Scrutiny recommendations are tracked through to implementation and the outcome reported back to the panel. They cite the credibility of the “you said, we did” cycle as the primary reason tenant participation rates have increased year on year.

Sovereign Network Group (England)

After historically low engagement rates, Sovereign redesigned their approach around the question “what stopped people engaging last time?” rather than “how do we get more responses to our survey?”. The answer from tenants was: surveys feel like they go nowhere; we never hear what changed as a result. They rebuilt their feedback process around the visible closing of the loop and saw participation rates more than double within 18 months.

East Renfrewshire Council — Own Experience

ERC has run tenant engagement exercises before with mixed results. The lessons from those experiences are assets — what worked, what didn’t, what tenants said, where participation dropped off. These should be the starting point for designing the engagement approach for this programme, not set aside in favour of a generic engagement plan.

Questions to Keep Asking

  1. What did past tenant engagement tell us that we didn’t already know? What did we do with it?
  2. What stopped tenants engaging last time? What is specifically different about this programme’s approach?
  3. How will tenants know that their feedback changed something? What is the “you said, we did” mechanism?
  4. What do tenants most want to know about their repair, and at which points? Have we asked them, or are we guessing?
  5. Are we making engagement genuinely accessible to tenants who are elderly, disabled, speak English as a second language, or are digitally excluded? Or are we designing engagement for the easiest people to reach?
  6. Who in the organisation is accountable for tenant engagement on this programme, and do they have enough time and resource to do it properly?
  7. Is the tenant scrutiny panel being given real data and real influence, or is it a consultation exercise with a predetermined outcome?
