Tenant Engagement — The Methodology

The Scottish Approach to Service Design

The Scottish Approach to Service Design (SAtSD) is the methodology adopted by the Scottish Government and embedded across ERC to ensure public services are designed with the people who use them, not just for them. It moves the starting point from what the organisation wants to build to what the user actually needs — and it provides a structured way of finding that out.

ERC trained: 100+ staff in SAtSD and user research via Scottish Digital Academy
Source: gov.scot/publications/scottish-approach-service-design

ERC already has this capability

SAtSD is not an external framework we need to introduce to ERC. It is already embedded across the organisation. The Housing Digital Transformation Project is stepping into a council that has trained over 100 people in this methodology, has delivered multiple co-designed services using it, and has a Digital Champions network experienced in supporting people through change. The task is to apply what already works to the housing context.

What SAtSD Is

The core shift: outside-in thinking

Historically, public services have been designed from the inside out: the organisation decides what it wants to offer, builds it, then tells people about it. SAtSD reverses this. It starts with user needs — what problems do people actually have, what do they actually need to achieve, and where does the current service fail them?

For housing digital transformation, this means starting not with “what system should we buy?” but with “what does a tenant actually need when something goes wrong with their home?” The technology choice follows from the answer — it does not precede it.

ERC's transformation strategy explicitly names this shift from “inside-out” service provision to “outside-in” service co-creation as one of its core commitments.

Value co-created with users

SAtSD operates from the principle that public services only create real value when that value is experienced by the people using the service. A repairs portal that housing officers love but tenants find confusing has not created value. A system that reduces admin time but increases the number of calls tenants have to make to chase repairs has not created value.

This framing is directly relevant to the business case for housing digital transformation. The benefits case needs to measure outcomes that tenants experience — faster repairs, clearer communication, fewer calls to chase — not just internal efficiency metrics.

The Double Diamond — Applied to Housing

The Double Diamond is the design process framework at the heart of SAtSD. It takes its name from the shape made by two diverging-then-converging stages: first understanding the problem, then developing the solution. The critical principle is that you do not start developing solutions until you have properly understood the problem.

◆ Discover

Diverge: open up the problem space

Research with tenants, housing staff, and repair operatives to understand the current experience from every angle. What actually happens when a tenant reports a repair? Where does it break down? What matters most to different tenant groups? What do staff find frustrating about the current process?

For our programme: tenant interviews, shadow days, journey mapping workshops, Citizens Panel questions on housing satisfaction.

◆ Define

Converge: define the right problem to solve

Synthesise the research to define the problem clearly. What are the top three things that tenants need to be different? Where is the biggest gap between current experience and acceptable experience? This definition stage prevents the common mistake of solving the wrong problem very efficiently.

For our programme: insight reports, problem statements signed off with tenants and housing teams, evidence-based brief for system requirements.

◆ Develop & Deliver

Diverge then converge: build and test solutions

Prototype potential solutions with tenants before building. Test early. Iterate based on what does and does not work. Deliver incrementally so tenants experience improvements throughout the programme, not only at the end. Each iteration is informed by real user feedback.

For our programme: usability testing of portal prototypes, remote testing sessions, phased rollout with feedback loops at each stage.

The Five Principles — What They Mean for Housing

Design with people, not for them

The user is a participant in the design process, not just a recipient of its output.

In housing, this means tenants are actively involved in designing the repairs reporting journey, the appointment notification process, and the self-service portal — not just consulted on a finished product. It means running co-design workshops where tenants prototype solutions alongside housing staff, not just attending briefings. ERC's own data shows this works: 90% of new parents indicated they preferred digital booking over telephone calls, and the nursery admissions system was co-designed around that preference rather than built first and adapted later.

Design end-to-end processes

Map the entire user journey, not just individual transactions.

A tenant reporting a repair is not completing a transaction — they are beginning a journey that includes reporting, confirmation, appointment booking, the visit, the repair, and the follow-up. Every step in that journey needs to work. A brilliant repairs portal that feeds into a slow, opaque job management process has not solved the tenant's problem. SAtSD requires us to map the full journey — including the parts that involve contractors, technical officers, and back-office systems — before deciding what to digitise.

Design for inclusion, not just access

Digital-first must never mean digital-only.

ERC's housing stock includes tenants who are elderly, who have disabilities, who have limited English, and who have low digital confidence or no internet access. A digital-first approach that does not actively accommodate these groups will make those tenants worse off, not better. SAtSD requires that inclusive design is built in from the start — not added as an afterthought. For housing digital transformation, this means conducting research with digitally excluded tenants specifically, not assuming their needs are covered by the general population.

Iterate based on evidence

Design decisions should be driven by what users do, not what they say they will do.

ERC used Microsoft Teams to run live, collaborative testing of the Council Tax and Benefits system with real users during the pandemic — before go-live. This is exactly the right instinct. For housing, the same approach applies: before the new repairs portal goes live, tenants should be testing it. Before appointment notifications are designed, tenants should be shown prototypes. Each round of testing feeds directly into the design. The agile delivery model that ERC already uses aligns naturally with this iterative approach.

Build capacity, not dependency

The programme should leave ERC more capable, not dependent on external expertise.

SAtSD is not just a project methodology — it is a way of building institutional capability. When the Housing Digital Transformation Project ends, ERC's housing team should be able to conduct user research, run usability testing, and iterate services without bringing in consultants. ERC has already built this capability across other services through the Scottish Digital Academy. The Housing Digital Transformation Project should use that existing network, not create a parallel structure. The Digital Champions embedded across the organisation are a direct resource for supporting tenants through digital transitions.

What This Means for the Housing Digital Transformation Programme

What we will do differently because of SAtSD

  • Run a discovery phase before any system selection, focused on understanding the current tenant experience of repairs, housing management, and communication
  • Establish a tenant design panel representative of the housing stock — including older tenants, disabled tenants, and those with limited digital access — who participate throughout the programme
  • Test prototypes with tenants before building; iterate based on what testing reveals rather than internal assumptions
  • Map the end-to-end repair journey from the tenant's perspective before deciding what to digitise, so we do not improve one part of the process while making another worse
  • Measure success from the tenant's perspective: time to resolution, number of contacts needed, tenant satisfaction, and self-service adoption rates

What happens if we skip this

  • We build what seems logical internally but does not match how tenants actually think about and describe their housing issues
  • The self-service portal launches with low adoption; tenants continue calling, negating the efficiency case
  • Digital exclusion is not surfaced until after go-live, when it is expensive to address and reputationally damaging
  • Tenant representatives become adversarial rather than advocates; the programme faces organised opposition
  • The Scottish Housing Regulator finds insufficient evidence of tenant participation; regulatory risk increases
  • We deliver a technically functional system that does not improve the tenant experience — the most common outcome of public sector digital transformation that skips the user research stage
