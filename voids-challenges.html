Voids — Challenges

Why Voids Are Hard to Fix

Even well-run void processes hit the same recurring blockers, and most of them sit outside a landlord’s direct control: external suppliers, market conditions, and a genuine tension between the needs of an existing tenant and the needs of the person waiting for an empty home. Understanding these constraints matters because it shapes what a digital solution can realistically fix — and what it can only make visible.

The honest framing

Not every delay is a process failure. Some of the longest void periods are caused by third parties — utility companies chief among them — who sit entirely outside a housing management system. A digital transformation programme can make these delays visible and trackable far faster than it can eliminate them. That distinction should shape expectations from the outset.

The Single Biggest External Blocker: Utility Companies

How the delay happens

  • Meter resets — the physical exchange or reset of meters is a frequently reported bottleneck; it must happen before a property can be safely and legally let
  • Debt clearance — debt left on a meter by the previous tenant is a common cause of delay, often taking weeks or months for the utility provider to resolve
  • Supplier switches & new installations — associations attempting to change supplier or requiring new energy infrastructure work face further delay
  • Legal inability to let — because utilities are essential services, a property simply cannot be let while these issues are pending — regardless of how fast the repairs themselves were completed

Why it matters

This has been flagged by Scotland’s Minister for Housing and multiple social landlords as a “particular challenge” that undermines the “whole-systems approach” needed to bring void turnaround times down nationally. It is a reminder that void performance cannot be entirely solved from inside a housing management system — it needs active relationship and SLA management with external suppliers, and a way to surface these specific delays separately from internal repair delays so they don’t get blamed on the wrong team.

Rising material costs and shortages of tradespeople have pushed re-let times up compared with pre-pandemic levels across the sector. Some landlords have gone as far as pausing non-urgent repairs for existing tenants to free up contractor capacity for void backlogs — a decision that reduces one form of harm (empty homes during a housing emergency) at the direct cost of another (existing tenants waiting longer for repairs). This trade-off, not a lack of effort, is often the real constraint behind headline turnaround figures.

“Low Demand” Properties

A smaller but disproportionately costly problem: properties that are repeatedly refused, or have little to no waiting list, and can sit empty for far longer than the standard void period regardless of condition.

  • Early warning signs — small waiting lists or high refusal rates are detectable in Housing Management System data well before a property becomes empty, if the data is being looked at proactively rather than reactively
  • Area perception — poor environmental conditions (untidy communal areas, disrepair on neighbouring blocks) can make an individual property hard to let even when the unit itself is sound
  • A distinct problem from the standard void — the interventions that fix a normal turnaround delay (faster repairs, incentives) do very little for a genuinely low-demand property; it needs marketing, incentives, environmental improvement, or in the most severe cases reconfiguration or disposal

The Recharge Grey Area

When a tenant leaves a property in poor condition, the cost of putting it right is often recharged to them — but this is a frequent source of dispute and delay in itself, not a clean administrative process.

What typically gets recharged — and why it’s contentious

Removal of rubbish and belongings, redecoration, damaged doors and lost keys are routinely billed back to the outgoing tenant if the property isn’t left in a lettable state. Landlords generally require at least 50% of the cost upfront before work starts, unless there’s an immediate safety risk — which itself can hold up the void while payment is negotiated or disputed. A recharge policy that is unclear or inconsistently applied doesn’t just create tenant friction; it directly extends turnaround time.
