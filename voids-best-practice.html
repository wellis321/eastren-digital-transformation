Voids — What Works & What Doesn't

Approaches Worth Adopting — and Approaches to Avoid

Reducing void turnaround is not one intervention, it’s a system of four working together: digital data infrastructure, integrated staff accountability, tenant involvement, and honest handling of stock that is genuinely low-demand. Several approaches that look productive on paper actually make the problem worse in practice. Both sides matter equally here — this page sets out what the evidence supports, and what to actively avoid.

Our position on this

The strongest performers in this space don’t win on any single tactic — they win because data, roles, and tenant relationships all point the same direction. A benchmarking dashboard with no dedicated owner changes nothing. A tenant incentive with no data behind it can’t be evaluated. We should treat these four areas as one connected design, not a menu to pick one item from.

What Works

Digital Infrastructure & Data

  • Benchmark externally — compare re-let performance and cost against peers through SHN, rather than judging progress only against last year’s internal figures
  • Use HMS data to predict, not just record — capture pre- and post-inspection data to spot “low demand” warning signs (small waiting lists, high refusal rates) before a property becomes empty
  • Categorise voids by complexity — targeted timescales (e.g. 3/5/10/12-day bands) let staff prioritise resource instead of treating every void identically

Integrated Staff Workflows

  • One accountable owner per void — a dedicated Void Property Officer role prevents cases falling between Housing and Property teams
  • Monitor daily, not weekly — catching slippage early is far cheaper than recovering a case that has already gone off-track
  • Meet regularly across departments — scheduled Housing/Property sessions specifically to resolve bottlenecks such as utility delays, rather than escalating ad hoc

Tenant Involvement & Incentives

  • Engage from the moment notice is given — pre-termination inspections let staff identify repairs and set expectations while the tenant is still in the property, not after
  • Reward good handovers — financial incentives (from a modest £50 procedural reward to a larger condition-based allowance) reduce recharges and speed re-let
  • Let tenants choose — Choice Based Lettings improves tenancy sustainment and reduces future turnover compared with allocation-only models
  • Co-design the standard — involving tenants in setting the Lettable Standard reduces refusals once a property is offered

Genuinely Low-Demand Stock

  • Market it properly — advertising and incentives (rent-free periods, decoration/furniture allowances) for the specific properties that are hardest to let
  • Fix the surroundings, not just the unit — tidying gardens, fixing gates, painting railings measurably improves perception of “difficult to let” blocks
  • Know when to stop repeating the same fix — reconfiguration, stock transfer, or in the most severe cases disposal, when a property is no longer viable at all

What Doesn't Work — and Why It's Tempting Anyway

Each of these looks like a reasonable response to pressure. All of them either fail to reduce turnaround, or solve one problem by quietly creating another.

Treating every void the same

Running one undifferentiated repairs queue for voids — no categorisation by complexity — means a straightforward “clean and check” property waits behind a full kitchen rebuild. Categorisation isn’t bureaucracy for its own sake; without it, the properties that could turn around fastest are the ones that get stuck.

Shared ownership with no single accountable person

When a void case is “everyone’s job” across Housing and Property, it is often no one’s priority. The evidence consistently points to a single named officer per case as the difference between steady progress and cases quietly stalling for weeks.

Leaving tenant engagement until the keys are handed back

If the first conversation about condition and responsibilities happens after the tenant has already left, the opportunity to influence the outcome is gone. Pre-termination inspection during the notice period is what actually shortens the void — engaging afterwards is just documenting a problem you can no longer prevent.

Leaning on recharges instead of prevention

Recharging a tenant for a poor handover recovers some cost, but it does nothing for turnaround time and is a frequent source of dispute that can itself delay a case. Treating recharge policy as the primary lever — rather than a backstop behind clear communication and incentives — produces friction without producing speed.

Pausing repairs for existing tenants without naming the trade-off

Some landlords have redirected contractor capacity away from non-urgent repairs to clear void backlogs. This can be a legitimate short-term call during a housing emergency — but only if it is a deliberate, time-boxed, communicated decision. Doing it silently as a workaround just moves the pressure from empty homes onto occupied ones, and erodes trust with tenants who see their repairs slip with no explanation.

Applying the same fix to a low-demand property as a standard void

Faster repairs and a tenant incentive scheme won’t rent out a property nobody wants to live in. Treating a genuinely low-demand unit as an ordinary turnaround delay wastes contractor time and disguises a problem — poor area perception, unsuitable layout, unviable condition — that needs a completely different response.

Ignoring utility delays in process design

If a housing management system only tracks internal repair stages, a property can look “complete” while it is legally unlettable due to a pending meter reset. Void tracking needs an explicit stage for third-party utility dependencies, with its own SLA and escalation route, or the reported turnaround figures will systematically understate the real delay.

Bringing It Together

A connected, not piecemeal, approach

Before committing to a specific digital solution, we should establish where we currently sit using SHN benchmarking data, then design around the four pillars together: void categorisation built into the housing management system, a single named officer per case with daily visibility, a tenant engagement and incentive model that starts at the notice period rather than the handover, and an explicit, tracked stage for third-party utility dependencies. This mirrors the wider approach we’re already taking to tenant engagement and repairs digitisation elsewhere in this programme — the same discipline of designing around the real workflow, not just adding a dashboard on top of the existing one.
