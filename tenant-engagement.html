Tenant Engagement

Why Meaningful Engagement Can Make or Break Digital Transformation Programmes and Projects

Research into digital transformation in social housing consistently shows the same finding: programmes that engage tenants early and genuinely succeed. Those that treat engagement as a communications exercise — telling tenants what is happening rather than designing with them — face resistance, low adoption, and in some cases outright failure. This is not a soft aspiration. It is the central risk to manage.

Tenants affected: 3,141 households across ERC housing stock
Legal basis: Scottish Secure Tenancy, Housing to 2040, SHR requirements

Our position on this

Tenant engagement is not a box to tick at the end of the programme. If we design a new repairs system, a new tenant portal, or a new communications model without the active involvement of the people who will use them, we will build something technically functional that does not work in practice. We want to build the engagement model into the programme from day one — not as an add-on, but as the primary source of evidence for what good looks like.

What the Evidence Shows

Why Digital Transformation Fails in Social Housing

Analysis of digital transformation programmes in UK social housing identifies a consistent set of failure causes. Poor tenant engagement runs through almost all of them:

  • Low adoption of self-service portals — tenants who were not involved in design do not trust or understand the new tools, and revert to phone contact, negating the business case
  • High failure demand — systems designed without user input create confusion that generates more contact, not less
  • Digital exclusion amplified — moving services online without understanding which tenants cannot access them leaves vulnerable households without support
  • Resistance from tenant representatives — tenant scrutiny panels and associations become adversarial when they feel changes are done to them, not with them
  • SHR intervention risk — the Scottish Housing Regulator expects landlords to demonstrate genuine tenant engagement in service change; absence of this is a regulatory risk

What Good Engagement Delivers

When tenant engagement is done well, the evidence points consistently to better outcomes:

  • Higher portal adoption rates — tenants who helped shape a system are more likely to use it; ERC's own work shows this with the 90% of new parents who chose digital booking once the option was co-designed with them
  • Reduced failure demand — services designed around real user journeys resolve issues first time, reducing repeat contacts and administration
  • Stronger trust in the landlord — evidence from the Scottish Housing Regulator's Tenant Satisfaction surveys shows that tenants who feel heard give higher overall satisfaction scores, regardless of service quality
  • Digital inclusion built in — engagement surfaces digital barriers early, when it is still possible to design around them, rather than after go-live
  • Regulatory confidence — a well-documented engagement trail demonstrates compliance with statutory tenant participation duties

The Legal and Regulatory Mandate

This is not optional

Tenant participation in service design is a condition of Scottish Secure Tenancy, reinforced by the Housing (Scotland) Act 2001. The Scottish Housing Regulator's Regulatory Framework requires registered social landlords to demonstrate how they involve tenants in decisions about their services. Meaningful engagement is not best practice — it is a statutory obligation.

Housing (Scotland) Act 2001

Requires social landlords to develop and publish a Tenant Participation Strategy. Any significant change to services — including the introduction of new digital channels — must be subject to meaningful tenant consultation. The standard is genuine influence, not notification.

Scottish Housing Regulator

The SHR's Regulatory Framework assesses landlords against the Scottish Social Housing Charter. Charter Outcome 3 specifically covers tenant and resident participation. SHR expects landlords to demonstrate how tenant views have actually shaped services — a paper engagement trail is not sufficient.

Housing to 2040

Scotland's 25-year housing strategy explicitly names tenant participation as a condition of a well-managed home. It reinforces the direction of travel: tenants as partners in housing services, not passive recipients. This directly frames how we should design our digital transformation programme.

What ERC Has Already Demonstrated

ERC has a genuine track record in co-design and citizen engagement through the Scottish Approach to Service Design. These are not abstract commitments — they are things that have happened and produced measurable outcomes. The Housing Digital Transformation Project can build on this foundation.

Real examples of engagement shaping services

  • Council Tax and Benefits portal — remote user testing via Microsoft Teams during the 2020 pandemic captured genuine citizen feedback before go-live, producing a streamlined portal that reduced manual intervention
  • Nursery admissions — 90% of new parents expressed a preference for digital booking over telephone; this directly shaped the new online system rather than just adding a digital channel on top of the old process
  • Dams to Darnley country park — engagement led to specific changes in consent forms and the addition of real-time ticket availability, demonstrating that feedback produced tangible design changes, not just notes in a report
  • Environment and waste services — direct customer feedback drove the migration of missed bin reporting to the digital platform, reducing manual processing and customer frustration simultaneously

The infrastructure already in place

  • Citizens Panel — an existing mechanism for surveying resident opinion at scale; can be used to understand tenant attitudes to digital services, preferred contact channels, and barriers to adoption
  • Over 100 staff trained in SAtSD — ERC has in-house capability in user research and service design; the Housing Digital Transformation Project does not need to import this from scratch
  • Digital Champions network — 30+ staff members who support both colleagues and tenants through digital transitions; a ready-made resource for the inclusion challenge
  • IoT housing pilots — air quality sensors in 50 properties have already begun a data-informed relationship with tenants; this creates a natural opening for deeper engagement about what data means for them

Tokenistic vs. Meaningful Engagement

Not all engagement is equal. There is a significant difference between informing tenants about changes and genuinely involving them in shaping those changes. The table below sets out the distinction clearly.

Dimension Tokenistic engagement Meaningful engagement
When it happens After key decisions are made, to communicate outcomes Before and during design, to shape decisions
What tenants are asked “Do you like this?” (on something already built) “What problem are we trying to solve for you?”
Who is involved The same vocal representatives; committee meetings A diverse cross-section, including seldom-heard groups
What happens to feedback Noted and reported; rarely traceable to changes Directly traceable to specific design decisions
Accessibility Evening meetings in council buildings Multiple formats, times, and channels; digital and in-person
Result Compliance on paper; low trust; low adoption Services that work; higher adoption; stronger relationships
