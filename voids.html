Voids

Why Void Management Is a Digital Transformation Priority

A “void” is a property with no current tenancy — empty, and generating no rent, until it is repaired, safety-checked, and re-let. It sounds like an operational detail. Across the Scottish social housing sector it is a £1.6 billion problem, and it sits at the exact intersection of the things this programme is meant to fix: fragmented data, manual workflows, and processes that were never designed around the tenant or the property lifecycle. Voids deserve the same digital transformation attention as repairs and tenant engagement — not a separate conversation, but one strand of the same fabric.

ERC stock: 3,141 units under the Housing Asset Management Plan (HAMP 2024–2026)
Legal basis: Housing (Scotland) Act 2001, Right to Repair Regulations 2002, Housing (Scotland) Act 2006 (Repairing Standard), SHQS

Our position on this

Voids are not just a repairs backlog to clear — they are a symptom of how well data, roles, and tenant relationships work together across the whole tenancy lifecycle. A property does not become a long void because one thing failed; it is usually several small failures compounding: no early inspection, no clear ownership, no visibility of where the delay actually sits, and no incentive for either party to help. That is a systems and data problem before it is a trades problem, which is exactly why it belongs in this programme.

What a Void Actually Costs

Every day a property sits empty is a day of lost rent, plus the repair, cleaning, and letting costs needed to bring it back into use. The sector-wide numbers make the case for treating this as a priority, not a footnote.

The Legal and Regulatory Framework

Void management is a regulated activity, not just an operational one

Every stage of the void process — from the repair timescales applied, through to the standard a property must meet before re-let — sits inside a specific Scottish statutory and regulatory framework. Digital tools have to be built around these requirements, not bolted on afterwards.

Right to Repair

Housing (Scotland) Act 2001 and the Scottish Secure Tenants (Right to Repair) Regulations 2002 give tenants in an occupied property the right to compensation if qualifying urgent repairs miss set timescales. It doesn’t govern void turnaround directly — that runs on internal target timescales — but it’s part of the same repairs framework a void inspection has to work within.

Repairing Standard & SHQS

The Housing (Scotland) Act 2006 sets the Repairing Standard, and properties must also meet the Scottish Housing Quality Standard. A void cannot legally be re-let until both are satisfied — the “Lettable Standard” landlords apply internally is built on top of this baseline.

Scottish Housing Regulator

The SHR monitors performance against the Scottish Social Housing Charter, including average re-let times and the percentage of rental income lost to void properties. Landlords submit an Annual Assurance Statement confirming they meet safety, performance and financial requirements — the SHR can require a Performance Improvement Plan if Charter outcomes are not met.

What Actually Happens During a Void

The Void Period, Step by Step

  • Notice given — a tenant typically gives 28 days’ notice; a pre-termination inspection identifies repairs and communicates responsibilities before the keys are even returned
  • Keys returned — the “void period” formally begins: the property generates no rent from this point
  • Safety checks — mandatory gas and electrical safety inspections must be completed before anyone can move in
  • Repairs & cleaning — rubbish and contractor waste removed, doors/windows/plumbing/heating brought to a functional standard
  • Re-let — the property meets the Lettable Standard and is allocated to a new tenant

The Lettable Standard

Before a property can be re-let, it must meet a defined internal standard built on the statutory baseline:

  • Safety — gas safety and electrical inspections, both mandatory
  • Cleanliness — all rubbish, furniture and contractor waste removed
  • Functionality — windows, doors, plumbing and heating wind- and watertight, and working
  • Tenant involvement — some landlords involve tenants in reviewing and setting the standard itself, to keep it realistic and reduce refusals once a property is offered
